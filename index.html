Why the bell curve's area is the square root of pi

What you're looking at: A 3D hill made by spinning the bell curve round. Its height is the bell curve in one direction times the bell curve in the other. We measure how much clay is in it, two different ways.

Why it matters: The bell curve is everywhere, from exam scores to noise in a photo, and you constantly need its area. But no ordinary formula has it as its slope, so the usual way of finding areas fails. The trick: build the 3D hill. Cut into slabs, it holds (area) × (area). Peeled into rings, each ring is easy to add up, because 2π r e^(−r²) is exactly the slope of something simple. The total is π. So area × area = π, and the area is the square root of π. That's where the square root of 2π in every bell-curve formula comes from. (Switching from slabs to rings is the change to polar coordinates; the r in 2π r is the Jacobian: how much room a ring has.)

How to use it

  1. Drag Number of slices: both dials settle on the same number as the cuts get finer.
  2. Press Peel into rings: each ring unrolls into the tray and the total climbs to π.
  3. Press Cut into slabs to go back: every slab is a smaller bell curve.
  4. Make the bell wider: the volume grows as π × width², the area as width × square root of π.
  5. Reset to example puts everything back.
